01 — First, take a breath

Most orthodontic scares aren't emergencies.

Rare · Usually manageable at home · One phone call away

A wire starts poking your cheek at 9 p.m. A bracket pops loose halfway through a movie. It feels urgent, and it's genuinely uncomfortable — but here's the truth we share with patients almost every week: true orthodontic emergencies are rare. Nearly every braces or aligner problem is an annoyance rather than an injury, and almost all can be made comfortable tonight and repaired at a normal appointment.

What we ask is simple. Call our Round Rock office at (512) 828-7900 and tell us what happened. During office hours we'll talk you through first aid and find you a repair slot. If we're closed, follow the voicemail instructions — leave your name, a callback number, and a short description, and our team will follow up. Patients of Dr. Asrar should start with our main line rather than urgent care for a loose bracket.

  • Severe, escalating pain — pain that over-the-counter relief doesn't touch, or that keeps worsening.
  • Swelling — a puffy cheek, jaw, or gum, especially with fever or a foul taste.
  • Trauma to the face or teeth — a fall or blow that chips, moves, or loosens a tooth.
  • Bleeding that won't stop — after 10 to 15 minutes of steady pressure.
  • Trouble breathing or swallowing — or a swallowed appliance piece. That's a medical emergency, not an orthodontic one.
  • Everything else — poking wires, loose brackets, sore spots, cracked trays: safe to manage until we see you.

02 — What actually goes wrong

The short list of common problems.

Wires · Brackets · Trays · Sore spots

Across our patients in Round Rock, Cedar Park, Georgetown, and North Austin, nearly every call falls into one of the categories below. If yours isn't listed, call anyway — we'd rather answer a two-minute question than leave you worrying.

  • Poking or broken wire — a loose end rubbing your cheek or tongue.
  • Loose or broken bracket — usually a hard or sticky food to blame.
  • Lost ligature or o-ring — the tiny tie that holds the wire in its slot.
  • Loose band — the metal ring cemented around a back molar.
  • Soreness after an adjustment — expected, and usually gone in a few days.
  • Mouth sores — cheeks and lips toughening up to new hardware.
  • Food caught in braces — worth clearing properly, not waiting out.
  • Teeth that feel loose — unsettling, but teeth must loosen to move.
  • Broken or lost retainer — a real risk to your finished result.
  • Cracked aligner or a tray that won't seat — common mid-series.
  • Appliance or expander problems — a loose expander, bent arm, or stuck key.
  • Cut lip, cheek, or tongue — usually after a knock to the face.

03 — Emergency by emergency

What to do, step by step.

Home first aid · When to call · What to bring

Open the situation that matches yours — each one says whether it's urgent and what to do in the next ten minutes. First, though, stock a small kit. It fits in a drawer, and it's the difference between a stressful evening and a minor inconvenience.

  • Orthodontic wax — dry the spot, roll a pea-sized ball, press it over the sharp edge. Sugarless gum works in a pinch.
  • A clean pencil eraser or cotton swab — for tucking a stray wire end flat.
  • Salt for warm rinses — half a teaspoon in a cup of warm water.
  • Over-the-counter pain relief — as the label directs, plus a cold compress.
  • Floss threaders or a water flosser — for trapped food. Never a toothpick.
  • Clean nail clippers — a last resort for a wire too long to tuck or wax. Sterilize them; save the piece.
Soreness or a toothache after an adjustment

Is it an emergency? Almost never. Aching when you bite is expected for a few days after new braces, a tightening, or a new set of trays — it's teeth moving.

What to do: Pain relief as directed, a cold compress, warm salt-water rinses, soft foods. Call us if the pain sharpens, focuses on one tooth, or lingers past a few days.

Poking or protruding wire

Is it an emergency? No — but don't live with it for weeks. Constant rubbing wears a sore that can become infected.

What to do: Press the wire flat with a clean pencil eraser or swab, then wax the end. As a last resort, trim it with sterilized nail clippers — tissue tucked behind it, save the piece — and call us.

Loose bracket

Is it an emergency? No. It rarely hurts, but that tooth stops moving until the bracket is re-bonded, so weeks of waiting can stretch your treatment.

What to do: Leave it threaded on the wire, wax it if it spins, save any piece that falls off, and call for a repair slot.

Broken bracket or broken wire

Is it an emergency? More pressing than a loose one: a broken edge can cut soft tissue. Don't reshape or repair it yourself.

What to do: Wax every sharp edge, collect the fragments, avoid chewing on that side, and call us right away.

Lost ligature or o-ring

Is it an emergency? No. Losing the tiny elastic ring or wire tie won't hurt, though that tooth loses full pressure.

What to do: Press a stray tie flat or wax it — never replace an o-ring yourself. Call us and mention the tooth; it's a two-minute fix.

Loose band on a back tooth

Is it an emergency? Not quite, but sooner than a bracket: the cement seal has broken, and the enamel underneath is less protected.

What to do: Don't push it down or glue it. Save it if it comes off, skip sticky foods, and call us to have it re-cemented.

Mouth sores and irritation

Is it an emergency? No. Cheeks and lips toughen up over one to three weeks, and canker sores are common then.

What to do: Wax whatever rubs, rinse with warm salt water, use a numbing gel if you like. Call us if a sore grows or looks infected — our prevention tips help.

Food caught in your braces

Is it an emergency? No, but don't wait it out — trapped food invites decay and gum inflammation.

What to do: Rinse hard with warm water, then use a floss threader or water flosser. Never a toothpick; the tip can snap off or gouge your gum. Call us if it's truly wedged.

Cut lip, cheek, or tongue

Is it an emergency? Usually not, even though mouth cuts bleed dramatically. If bleeding continues after 10 to 15 minutes of firm pressure, go to the nearest emergency room.

What to do: Rinse gently, hold pressure with clean gauze, then use a cold compress and pain relief. Wax anything sharp and call us.

Teeth that feel loose

Is it an emergency? Rarely. Teeth loosen slightly in order to move, so mid-treatment wobble is normal — the biology is in why teeth feel loose with braces.

What to do: Don't wiggle or test them, and keep brushing and flossing immaculate. Call us if a tooth feels dramatically loose, has shifted, or your gums bleed.

Broken or lost retainer

Is it an emergency? Not medically — but it's the item most likely to cost you real ground: teeth drift within days without retention.

What to do: Never wear a cracked or warped retainer, and never glue one. Keep the pieces, retrace your steps, and call us the same or next business day. More on retention on our services page.

Cracked aligner, or a tray that won't fit

Is it an emergency? No. A hairline crack is often fine to keep wearing carefully; a split tray or one that won't seat needs a plan — see what to do if your tray won't fit.

What to do: Don't go without aligners and don't jump ahead on your own. Return to your previous set, keep the tray, never glue it, and call us. More on our Invisalign page.

Expander or appliance problems

Is it an emergency? Usually not, though a loose expander that rotates in the mouth needs same-day attention.

What to do: Stop turning the key and call us — forcing a turn on a loose appliance hurts. Wax any arm that digs in, and keep any piece that comes out. If a part is swallowed or inhaled, get medical care immediately.

04 — Prevention & limits

Fewer repairs, smoother treatment.

Mouthguards · Smart snacking · Regular checks

Every broken bracket costs you a repair visit and sometimes a little extra time in treatment. The same handful of habits prevents most of them.

  • Wear a mouthguard for sports — practices included. Ask us for one made to fit over brackets.
  • Skip hard foods — ice, hard candy, nuts, popcorn kernels, and raw carrots break the most brackets.
  • Skip sticky foods — caramel, taffy, gummies, and chewy granola bars pull brackets right off.
  • Cut food down — slice apples, corn off the cob, and sandwiches smaller.
  • Stop chewing non-food — pens, pencils, and fingernails are quiet appliance-killers.
  • Keep every checkup — a loose band caught at a routine visit is a two-minute fix.
  • Carry wax and a case — a hard case for aligners and retainers, never a napkin on a restaurant table.

What not to do — ever:

  • Never use superglue — on a bracket, band, retainer, or aligner. Household adhesives aren't safe in the mouth.
  • Never stop wearing your aligners — a damaged tray needs a plan, not a pause.
  • Never ignore a poking wire for weeks — wax buys you days, not a month.
  • Never wear a warped retainer — a bent or cracked one can move teeth the wrong way.
  • Never use a toothpick or any sharp tool — between brackets, wires, or teeth.
  • Never bend or reseat anything cemented — bands, expanders, and bonded wires are our job.

Go to the emergency room, not to us. A few situations are beyond what any orthodontic office is equipped for. For the three below, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room first, then let us know so we can repair your appliance.

Everything on this page is general guidance to keep you comfortable until you can be seen — it isn't a diagnosis or a substitute for being examined. Is a loose bracket an orthodontic emergency?

Almost never. It usually doesn't hurt and is safe to leave overnight or over a weekend — wax it if it spins and rubs, and stick to soft foods. It still needs fixing, because that tooth won't move until the bracket is re-bonded.

Can I cut a poking wire myself?

Tuck it first with a clean pencil eraser or cotton swab, then wax the end. Clipping is a last resort when you can't be seen soon: use sterilized nail clippers, tuck a tissue behind the wire so nothing can be swallowed, and call us afterward.

Can I glue a broken bracket, retainer, or aligner back together?

No. Household adhesives aren't safe inside the mouth, and they usually ruin a part we could have reused. Save every fragment in a bag, bring it in, and let us bond or replace it properly.

What do I do if your office is closed?

Nearly everything can be made comfortable for a night or a weekend with wax, warm salt-water rinses, a cold compress, and over-the-counter pain relief taken as directed. Call (512) 828-7900 and follow the voicemail instructions. For a true medical emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.

Is soreness after an adjustment normal, and will it go away?

Yes to both. Aching when you bite is the sensation of teeth moving, and it usually eases within three to five days. Soft foods, a cold compress, salt-water rinses, and pain relief before bed make it very manageable.

My aligner cracked — should I skip ahead to the next tray?

Not on your own. A hairline crack is often fine to keep wearing carefully, but if the tray has split or won't seat, return to your previous set and call us. Skipping ahead unsupervised is the most common way patients fall out of tracking.

Will a broken bracket make my treatment take longer?

One repaired promptly costs you very little. Several left unrepaired for weeks genuinely can add time, because those teeth stop receiving pressure while the rest keep moving. That's why we'd rather you call than wait for your next scheduled visit.

When should I go to the emergency room instead of calling you?

Go to the ER, or call 911, for facial trauma, bleeding that won't stop after 10 to 15 minutes of pressure, significant swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or a swallowed appliance piece. Once you've been seen, call us and we'll handle the appliance side.
