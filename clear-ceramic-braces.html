01 — What ceramic braces are

Braces that blend in.

Tooth-coloured brackets · Fixed · Full correction

Not everyone likes the idea of a silver smile, and not every case can be treated with trays. Clear ceramic braces answer both: translucent brackets tinted to match enamel, bonded to your teeth and moved by an archwire exactly as metal braces are. Not invisible, but easy to miss across a table.

Side by side the difference is plain: metal reads as a bright silver band that catches the light in photos, while ceramic takes the shade of the tooth behind it. Dr. Asrar uses 3M Clarity Advanced brackets, a system she helps evaluate on the 3M Innovative Orthodontics Advisory Board. They're smaller, lower-profile, and more rounded than older ceramics.

02 — How treatment works

Bonded once, adjusted often.

iTero scan · Bonding visit · Checks every 6–8 weeks

Treatment starts with a free consultation in Round Rock. Dr. Asrar examines your bite, reviews your X-rays, and takes an iTero digital impression — no putty. You'll leave with a timeline and written estimate.

At the bonding visit each bracket is placed and light-cured individually; Clarity Advanced brackets bond quickly, shortening that first long appointment. A thin archwire follows, held by clear or white ligatures. Then it's a short visit every six to eight weeks for a fresh wire and adjustments. Most cases run 18 to 24 months — mild crowding sooner, complex bites longer. Mechanically this is identical to traditional metal braces; only the look changes.

03 — Who it's for

A good fit for most adults.

Adults · Older teens · Upper front teeth

Ceramic braces suit patients who need fixed braces but don't want a year or two behind metal: professionals presenting to clients, students, image-conscious teens. Real crowding, rotated teeth, or a bite needing elastics point toward braces rather than trays.

  • You want a discreet look — Barely noticeable in person or in photos.
  • Your case is moderate to complex — Crowding, gaps, rotations, overbite, crossbite.
  • You brush and floss well — Bracket edges and clear ligatures show neglect fast.
  • You're not mid-season in contact sports — Doable with a mouthguard; ask first.

Two honest trade-offs. Ceramic is more brittle than steel, so a bracket is likelier to chip or pop off under a heavy bite. And while the brackets resist staining, the ligatures pick up coffee, tea, or curry — we swap those every visit, so it's temporary. That's why ceramic often goes on the upper front teeth, with metal on the lower and back teeth.

Metal is toughest and most affordable, Invisalign the most invisible but needs 20–22 hours of daily wear; ceramic sits between. Our guides on Invisalign vs. braces and when braces are necessary go deeper.

Do ceramic braces work as well as metal braces?

Yes — same bracket-and-archwire mechanics, so they correct the same crowding, spacing, and bite problems. The material changes, not the treatment.

Will my ceramic braces stain?

The brackets hold their shade. It's the ligatures that pick up coffee, tea, or curry, and we replace those every visit — so staining is temporary.

Do ceramic braces hurt more than metal?

No — the smaller, rounded brackets are gentler on lips and cheeks. Expect soreness for a few days after bonding and a tender day after each adjustment.

How long do ceramic braces take?

Most ceramic cases run 18 to 24 months. Mild crowding or a single arch finishes sooner; real bite correction takes longer. You'll get your own range after the exam.

Can teenagers get ceramic braces?

Often yes, especially older teens who look after their brushing. Ceramic is more brittle than steel, so we discuss contact sports and hard foods first; athletes wear a mouthguard.

Do ceramic braces cost more than metal braces?

A modest amount more, since the brackets cost more to place and remove. The $1,000-off special applies here too, and with $0 down and plans from $129 a month the gap is small.

Can ceramic brackets go on the bottom teeth too?

They can, though many patients do better with ceramic up front and metal on the lower and back teeth, where chewing force is strongest anyway.
